# Underwater

## The first watch

The cook has put two pieces of toast in the food lock, buttered face to face so the butter will melt. Ellis asks if you want yours now or after the run. Much later you will remember him asking this because it is the last thing on the tape before you hear somebody else finish your shift.

You are the working diver on the east jacket of Kestrel, a fictional fixed oil production platform in the northern North Sea. The bell hangs beside a bay of steel at a nominal depth of 126 metres. The surface and the bottom are both out of sight. Look up and a tube the width of a room disappears through green water. Look down and exactly the same thing happens. It is possible to become fond of a particular patch of yellow paint here.

An ordinary watch should be worth playing twice. An observant player should gradually become reluctant to let another diver take their place. The proposed full game spans one tour in saturation. The first playable build covers one repeatable bell run. All names, records and incidents are invented.

## A working day at pressure

The home of this crew is the saturation system aboard a diving support vessel alongside the installation. Chamber and closed bell connect under pressure. The crew can ride back onto the vessel, eat a hot meal and go to bed, while still living at storage pressure. Eventual decompression takes days. This gives the story a particularly uncomfortable kind of distance. There are people on the other side of a steel wall who can pass you a book but cannot open the door and shake your hand. [John Osborne's first-person account](https://www.aquamarinemedicals.co.uk/resources/life-as-a-saturation-diver)

1. **Wake and handover.** The outgoing team leaves a drawing, the latest readings and small practical advice. Meals and kit pass through locks. Before a bell run, the supervisor and bellman go through their checks. A half-finished remark about a fitting can be more important to the incoming diver than an entire tidy report. [Osborne](https://www.aquamarinemedicals.co.uk/resources/life-as-a-saturation-diver)
2. **Travel together, work out of sight.** A typical arrangement described by offshore contractor Boskalis is a team of three. One tends from the bell, two can work outside. The umbilical carries breathing gas, hot water and communications. Surface supervision and a life-support team make the apparently solitary image possible. In our crew Ellis tends at the bell, Noor supervises by wire, and Mara works around the far face. Hearing a colleague breathe while being unable to see her light is part of ordinary work. [Boskalis field account](https://magazine.boskalis.com/issue07/subsea)
3. **Earn the photograph.** Real offshore work includes identifying the specified joint, inspecting welds, taking photographs, checking wall thickness and installing mechanical fittings. The operator uses inspection plans to pick particular stress points. A clear reading from the wrong member is useless. Our puzzles compress acquiring and interpreting a reading or matching witness marks into gestures a thumb can perform. The instruments and their acceptance bands are fictional. [Maersk, Ensuring Production, pp. 11–12](https://www.maersk.com.cn/~/media_sc9/maersk/corporate/press/publications/files/2014-october-maersk-post-full-issue.pdf) · [Boskalis](https://magazine.boskalis.com/issue07/subsea) · [North Sea clamp and guide design](https://deesubsea.co.uk/clamp-design/)
4. **Come back through the same hole.** Kit, the bellman's hands and a debrief await at the bell. On the vessel the living system has bunks and space to wash and eat. Divers interviewed about saturation describe camaraderie, boredom, reading, films and the importance of a private routine. Model little intervals of domestic life. One crew member always rewinds the tape when somebody comes in halfway through a film. A repeated scene on the television can be perfectly innocent. Actual schedules vary by operation. [Osborne](https://www.aquamarinemedicals.co.uk/resources/life-as-a-saturation-diver) · [Interview study with saturation divers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2022.765197/full)

## The playable contract

The opening view is a suspended platform jacket and a working copy of the dive sheet. Start at the bell. Take either docket. Read the bay code, leave the bell and swim along the steel. Get within reach of the matching fixture and brace against it. Bracing steadies the tool view and frees both thumbs. Compare the instrument with what the docket asks for. File the result, find the bell and hand it in. Take the other job or return on another watch. Nothing expires while the diver looks around.

| Docket | What the hands do | What the eye learns | Satisfaction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A2 · Wall survey | Move the instrument gate across a pulse trace and set it over the first strong back-wall return after the near-face pulse. Record the reading. | Separate a reflection from surface noise and a later multiple. The geometry and scale stay legible while the readings vary. | A mark on the scale and a real number the relief can use. |
| B4 · Survey clamp | Adjust two ends of a removable carrier against separate witness marks. Seat the assembly when both ends line up. | One end can be true while the other sits high. The drawing shows which end needs attention. | The carrier sits flush and the station shows acceptance. |

A bad reading or a proud foot gets a specific observation from the instrument. Keep the setup and try again. A diver can leave the tool and keep exploring. Nobody grades speed. A fresh watch can revisit the same patch with different survey samples and carrier settings. Later bays should introduce new ways to read familiar equipment before they introduce new controls.

In the larger game a cleaned identification plate stays legible, a fitted carrier holds a lamp where you left it, and an older survey can be overlaid on a fresh reading. The rewards are better visibility, a familiar route, and confidence in your own record. Keep the first hours free of a money grind.

## The place and the crew

Build one vertical neighbourhood. On the east face, horizontal service bars carry paired cable runs and tags. Diagonals make different shapes at each intersection. The bell is the only large amber body in the water. Its downline goes straight up. The diver's excursion stays close to the structure and the working level. Neutral buoyancy arrests drift when input stops. In the full game a bell move stages a new working level. A long swim upward cannot take the place of a transfer under pressure.

Noor likes exact member IDs. She gives a bearing with reference to a fixture the diver can see. Ellis remembers who likes the jam left on the side of the plate. Mara is an experienced opposite-face diver with a habit of filing one corner off her survey shims so she can identify them by touch. She is the first person prepared to say she has seen something and the last prepared to guess what it means. The relief is someone the player can come to care for before the last handover. No member of the crew needs to be secretly malicious.

Give the chamber equal weight in the full game. Let the player label a tape, dry their cuffs over a familiar rail, add a sketch to the job book, or pull the thin curtain at their bunk. Meals come through the lock from an unseen cook. Let a whole visit pass with nothing uncanny. On the vessel time is marked by people doing things for one another.

## Story proposal · the relief face

Keep the following out of the first demo apart from a small optional note. The title of the game and the dive sheet should continue to sound like ordinary work.

**A discrepancy small enough to sign.** A weld photo from the prior shift shows a carrier just outside frame. You can find its shadow and its two clean witness marks, though the carrier itself is in your tool basket. Ellis says they probably lifted it between the photo and the handover. Most players will keep working. Those who manually compare their own photograph with the prior one will notice the file mark on Mara's shim has changed sides.

**The voluntary turn.** At supper Mara offers the other half of a stereo recording. Take it to the bench if you want. Align the two reels using the double knock of a basket landing. The free channel carries another crew working a few seconds beyond the end of your tape. This discovery unlocks a second layer on the inspection drawing. Nobody puts it on the job list.

**Learn a rule.** Only work that has been witnessed and signed for appears on the second face. An unrecorded chalk line stays singular. Mark a carrier, photograph it, then visit the apparently empty bay indicated by the second drawing. There is your mark, on a carrier with years of accumulated wear. Look back through the braces and your real work light is still on. The player can repeat this with an ordinary test piece. There is concrete evidence for the rule and considerable room for disagreement about the cause.

**What the older hands did.** Several generations of relief notes quietly pair real joint IDs with other positions. Some corrections kept workers from searching empty water. Others sent a diver to touch a fitting which had only ever appeared on paper. Older crews had already worked out that a diver can briefly occupy both records at handover. Their solution was to leave directions for the next person. The institution has kept the directions while forgetting what they were for.

**Last handover.** The outgoing player's name is already on a completed sheet in the relief's handwriting. You can leave the factual diagram and a personal instruction in the margin, or leave the diagram alone. In the chamber, listen to your relief do the first job from the game. If you left a note they recognise the misplaced carrier and call it by its proper name. If you didn't, they spend a long time describing it to a patient Ellis. Both sign the sheet. From inside the closed food lock, in your own voice, comes the first half of the next handover. Cut on the sound of the dog turning. The relief's log remains playable for ordinary watches afterward.

The player can pass on understanding and spare the next diver one bewildering experience. The process that reproduces the work and the worker continues. Leave open whether the archive has been making another platform, the second platform has been dictating the archive, or a person living at pressure simply moves between the two.

## Picture, controls and sound

Carry over Home Twenty's navigable silhouettes, short draw distance, low-frequency geometry, quiet grain and isolated warm lights. Use an ink-green water column, cold verdigris steel, marigold safety paint, faded enamel plates and cream instrument lettering. Near steel has enough corrosion to catch a grazing lamp. Repetition and the disappearance of the jacket above and below sell scale. Keep a light-adapted path between stations so phone players can navigate in a normally lit room. A tiny reticle, practical legends, a restrained work docket and big gloved-hand controls should feel like a diver's instruments. Use original generated surface photography and hand-built geometry.

Landscape phone is the lead platform. Left thumb moves, right thumb drags to look. Looking up or down while finning changes depth. Allow stopping to use a panel and one-handed operation of each instrument. Desktop uses WASD and mouse, with keyboard equivalents for context and leaving a panel. Give players a route back to the bell if they lose their bearings. Save signed work across sessions. Keep essential information on screen for players with audio muted.

Use the Home Twenty method of generating named ElevenLabs sound effects at authoring time, shaping and looping locally, then shipping audio with the build. Distinguish the bell machinery bed from breathing outside and damped tool contact. Let the sound of good contact be short enough that a diver enjoys making it twice. A far-off knock can be part of the structure settling. Save music for a time when removing it can change the room.

## Scope and playtest

The slice tests leaving the bell, finding two nearby fixtures, doing two kinds of skilled work, reading feedback, and handing over. It can suggest a larger crew through written comms and a prior-watch note. Chamber exploration, a campaign, the second face, recorded dialogue and the ending belong to subsequent builds. No timer, oxygen economy, enemy or forced pursuit belongs in this prototype.

For a first phone test, hand someone the bell sheet without explaining the controls. Watch where they look for A2, whether they can tell a bad echo from a good one after one correction, and whether they choose the other docket after handing in the first. Ask which landmark they used on the swim home and what they thought the relief meant. A person who enjoyed the carrier but missed the mystery is a successful first watch. A person who understood the story but would skip the next job means the tools need more work.
